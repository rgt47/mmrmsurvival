\begin{abstract}
Alzheimer's disease (AD) trials and oncology trials
analyze progressive disease in different ways. AD trials
mainly use the mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM)
on change from baseline. Oncology trials mainly use Cox
proportional hazards models on time to an event. We
review the history behind this divergence. We then
report a Monte Carlo simulation under the ADEMP
framework of Morris, White, and Crowther (2019). A
latent severity score is mapped to the global Clinical
Dementia Rating (CDR) stage. Participants enter at CDR
0.5, and dropout rises with latent severity. Three
analyses are fit to the same simulated trials. They are
MMRMs on the latent score and on the observed stage, and
a Cox model for time to CDR 1.0 or higher. No trial
observes the latent score, so its MMRM serves only as an
approximate upper bound. Secondary scenarios add a null
effect, a smaller effect, completely random dropout, and
a range of sample sizes. In the primary scenario, power
was highest for the latent-score MMRM and lowest for the
Cox model. The general advantage of continuous over
time-to-event analysis is known from Donohue et al.
(2011) and Li et al.~(2019). The contribution here is
narrower. The design separates the cost of a coarse
instrument from the cost of the time-to-event summary.
It does so under dropout that is informative for the Cox
model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsection{The divergent paths of clinical trial analysis}\label{the-divergent-paths-of-clinical-trial-analysis}

We begin with a simple observation. The statistical
analysis of clinical trials has taken quite different
paths in different therapeutic areas. In oncology,
time-to-event endpoints analyzed by survival methods
remain the regulatory standard. Overall survival is the
definitive measure of treatment efficacy\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fda2018oncology}{1},\citeproc{ref-fleming1996surrogate}{2}}. Alzheimer's
disease (AD) and other neurodegenerative disease trials,
on the other hand, have converged on change-from-baseline
estimands. The mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) is
their primary analytic framework\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-donohue2012mmrm}{3},\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodt2003assessing}{4}}. In our
view the divergence owes at least as much to regulatory
precedent as to differences in disease biology. It also
reflects the statistical properties of the available
endpoints. The review that follows is mainly a history of
that precedent.

In this paper we trace the historical developments behind
the current analytic conventions in each field. We then
present a simulation study comparing MMRM and survival
analysis on the same underlying disease process. The
Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale is the primary
measure.

\subsection{Regulatory origins: the FDA dual-outcome requirement}\label{regulatory-origins-the-fda-dual-outcome-requirement}

The 1990 FDA guidelines written by Paul Leber were
decisive in shaping the regulatory framework for AD
clinical trials. These guidelines required evidence of
efficacy on two co-primary endpoints. One was cognitive,
such as the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive
Subscale (ADAS-Cog). The other was a global or functional
measure\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-leber1990guidelines}{5},\citeproc{ref-rosen1984adas}{6}}. The
rationale was straightforward. A statistically significant
difference on a psychometric test alone might not reflect
clinically meaningful change. A second, independent
measure of global function was therefore needed to confirm
relevance. This dual-outcome requirement set a precedent
for how AD trials would be judged. Under it, trials would
be evaluated on the magnitude and direction of change from
baseline. The occurrence of discrete clinical events would
not be the measure of benefit.

Two instruments became the pillars of AD trial assessment.
These were the ADAS-Cog, developed by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rosen1984adas}{6}}, and
the CDR, codified by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris1993cdr}{7}}. The CDR in particular
provided a global staging instrument, the global CDR
score, with a range of 0 to 3. It also provided a
sum-of-boxes score (CDR-SB) with a range of 0 to 18. The
CDR-SB offered a quasi-continuous measure of disease
severity suited to change-score analysis. The 2018 FDA
guidance on early AD further reinforced the
change-from-baseline paradigm. At the same time, it
allowed more flexibility in endpoint selection for earlier
disease stages\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fda2018earlyad}{8}}.

\subsection{The fall of LOCF and the rise of MMRM}\label{the-fall-of-locf-and-the-rise-of-mmrm}

Through the 1990s, AD trials were usually analyzed with
last observation carried forward (LOCF). This was combined
with analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) at the final time
point. Under LOCF, a participant's last observed value is
imputed for all later missing visits. The analysis then
proceeds as if no data were missing. The approach was
computationally convenient and widely accepted by
regulators. However, it rests on the untenable assumption
of a flat trajectory after dropout. That assumption is
badly violated in progressive diseases. In these diseases,
dropout is often driven by worsening symptoms.

The foundational simulation work of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodt2001dropout}{9}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodt2001typei}{10}} showed
that LOCF produces substantially biased estimates of
treatment effect. LOCF also inflates Type I error rates
when dropout differs between treatment arms. The direction
of the bias depends on who leaves. Suppose that in a
progressive disease like AD more placebo participants
leave because of worsening. LOCF then freezes them at an
earlier, less impaired state. As a result, the estimated
treatment effect is attenuated. The opposite pattern is
also common. In symptomatic trials such as those of
cholinesterase inhibitors, the active arm often had more
dropout because of adverse events. Those participants may
leave while still relatively well. LOCF then preserves
their favorable scores and inflates the apparent benefit.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodt2003assessing}{4}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodt2003response}{11}}
extended this work by comparing LOCF with the mixed model
for repeated measures (MMRM). MMRM uses all observed data
in a likelihood-based framework. This framework is valid
when data are missing at random (MAR). Unlike LOCF, MMRM
does not impute missing values. Instead, it borrows
information from the observed correlation structure across
time points. This gives unbiased estimates under MAR.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-obrien2005semiparametric}{12}} proposed alternative
semi-parametric and non-parametric methods. They found
that MMRM performed comparably to their proposed
approaches while making less restrictive assumptions.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-molenberghs2004analyzing}{13}} provided the theoretical basis
for likelihood-based analyses of incomplete longitudinal
data.

By the mid-2000s, the weight of evidence against LOCF was
overwhelming. The 2010 National Research Council panel
report, commissioned by the FDA, formally recommended
against single imputation methods. It endorsed
likelihood-based and multiple imputation approaches as the
preferred alternatives\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-nrc2010missing}{14}}. The ICH E9(R1)
addendum on estimands further clarified the conceptual
framework. It required explicit specification of the
target estimand before an analytic method is chosen. That
specification includes how intercurrent events, such as
treatment discontinuation, are handled\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ich2019e9r1}{15}}.

\subsection{MMRM as the primary analysis in AD}\label{mmrm-as-the-primary-analysis-in-ad}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-donohue2012mmrm}{3}} compared MMRM, slope models, and
quadratic models across five AD Cooperative Study (ADCS)
trials. MMRM treats time as categorical and makes no
parametric assumptions about the mean trajectory. In their
comparison it gave robust estimates across a range of
disease stages and trial durations. Continuous-time
(slope) models can be more statistically efficient when
the linearity assumption holds. On the other hand, the
unstructured mean of MMRM offers protection against model
misspecification. Such protection is relevant in a disease
with heterogeneous and potentially nonlinear trajectories.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-wang2018dpm}{16}} proposed a disease progression model (DPM)
with proportional treatment effects. They showed power
gains over MMRM in autosomal dominant AD. However, the DPM
requires stronger parametric assumptions, and these may
limit generalizability.

Many recent pivotal AD trials have used MMRM or a close
variant as the pre-specified primary analysis. The
aducanumab EMERGE and ENGAGE trials analyzed change from
baseline in CDR-SB in this way\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-buddhaeberlein2022aducanumab}{17}}. So did the phase 3
lecanemab trial, Clarity AD\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vandyck2023lecanemab}{18}}.
The practice is not universal, however. The phase 2b
lecanemab trial, Study 201, had a Bayesian analysis of
change in ADCOMS at 12 months as its primary endpoint\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-swanson2021lecanemab}{19}}.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-dhadda2022lecanemab}{20}} later
examined whether its results were consistent across
clinical measures and statistical methods. The donanemab
trial TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 analyzed the integrated Alzheimer's
Disease Rating Scale (iADRS) with a natural cubic spline
model\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sims2023donanemab}{21}}. That trial also reported the
risk of progression to the next global CDR stage, a
time-to-event summary.

\subsection{Why survival analysis persists in oncology}\label{why-survival-analysis-persists-in-oncology}

In oncology, the natural endpoint is death. Overall
survival (OS) has been the primary measure of treatment
efficacy since the earliest cancer clinical trials. Death
is a binary, unambiguous, and universally meaningful
event.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fleming1996surrogate}{2}} cautioned against replacing OS with
surrogate endpoints without meeting rigorous validation
criteria. The FDA has historically required OS data for
full approval of cancer drugs, as opposed to accelerated
approval\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fda2018oncology}{1}}. Progression-free survival or
response rate may be the primary endpoint for accelerated
approval. Even then, confirmatory trials typically require
OS data as well.

The persistence of survival analysis in oncology reflects
several features that set it apart from AD. First, death
is a clearly defined, irreversible event. Second, the
proportional hazards framework is well understood and has
established regulatory precedent. Third, oncology has a
tradition of interim analyses based on information
fractions from event counts. Fourth, oncology has no
validated continuous severity measure that changes
monotonically and is universally accepted across tumor
types.

\subsection{Rate of change vs.~time to event in AD}\label{rate-of-change-vs.-time-to-event-in-ad}

The CDR and similar scales can define change scores, which
suit MMRM. They can also define threshold crossings, which
suit survival analysis. But how efficient is one approach
relative to the other on the same underlying data?

The question is not new to AD. Two of the larger
Alzheimer's Disease Cooperative Study trials used
time-to-event primary endpoints. The vitamin E and
selegiline trial in moderate AD analyzed time to a
composite of clinical milestones\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sano1997selegiline}{22}}.
The vitamin E and donepezil trial in amnestic MCI analyzed
time to a diagnosis of AD\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-petersen2005vitamine}{23}}. Data
from the first of these trials were later reanalyzed as
repeated measures, with attention to the missing data that
such measures bring\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-thomas2000longitudinal}{24}}. Milestone
endpoints need long follow-up and many events. Perhaps for
this reason, the field has since moved largely to change
scores. The efficiency argument we examine here is one
likely reason for that move.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-donohue2011relative}{25}} derived an analytic inflation factor
comparing Cox proportional hazards models of time to a
threshold with linear models of continuous outcomes. They
checked it by simulation from models fit to ADNI data. In
their setting the linear models consistently gave greater
power.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-li2019relative}{26}} extended this work to
presymptomatic AD. They simulated continuous outcomes from
a joint mixed model fit to ADNI and derived progression
events from the same trajectories. Power was roughly
doubled for the continuous analysis, including under
informative missingness.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-raket2022pmrm}{27}} introduced the
progression model for repeated measures (PMRM). The PMRM
expresses treatment effects in time units, such as months
of delay, and not in CDR-SB points.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-jonsson2024progression}{28}} applied it in AD and compared it
with traditional methods.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ito2014predicting}{29}} modeled
CDR-SB trajectories to predict time to clinically
meaningful worsening in MCI patients. Their estimates
inform both trial duration and sample size planning.

\subsection{Present study}\label{present-study}

Theoretical and empirical evidence favors continuous
measures, and our simulation does not add a new general
result. Its aim is narrower. We simulate a staged global
CDR from a latent severity process, with an entry
criterion of CDR 0.5 and severity-dependent dropout. The
survival endpoint is conversion from CDR 0.5 (MCI or very
mild dementia) to CDR 1.0 or higher (mild dementia). On
the same simulated trials we fit three analyses. The
first is an MMRM on the latent severity score. No trial
observes this score, so it serves as an approximate upper
bound. The
second is an MMRM on the observed global CDR stage. The
third is a Cox model for time to conversion. Comparing the
first and second analyses shows the cost of a coarse
instrument. Comparing the second and third shows the cost
of the time-to-event summary on the same instrument.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{ADEMP structure}\label{ademp-structure}

Following Morris, White, and Crowther\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019using}{30}}, the
simulation is reported under the ADEMP framework.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Aims.} Compare three analyses of the same simulated
  trials. Two are MMRMs, on change in a latent severity
  score and on change in the observed global CDR stage.
  The third is a Cox proportional hazards model for time
  to first global CDR \(\geq 1.0\).
\item
  \textbf{Data-generating mechanisms.} A mixed-effects model for
  latent severity, discretized into global CDR stages, with
  an entry criterion of CDR 0.5 and dropout; details below.
  The primary mechanism is supplemented by three secondary
  scenarios (null effect, smaller effect, and MCAR dropout)
  and by a sweep over sample size.
\item
  \textbf{Estimands.}

  \begin{enumerate}
  \def\labelenumi{(\roman{enumi})}
  \tightlist
  \item
    For each MMRM: the difference between arms in mean
    change from month 0 to month 30, expressed as the linear
    combination \(\beta_{\mathrm{trt}} + \beta_{\mathrm{trt:visit}_K}\).
  \item
    For Cox: the log hazard ratio for time to first
    CDR \(\geq 1.0\). True values are given under `Estimand
    values' below.
  \end{enumerate}
\item
  \textbf{Methods.} MMRM via \texttt{nlme::gls} with unstructured
  correlation and visit-specific variances; Cox via
  \texttt{survival::coxph}.
\item
  \textbf{Performance measures.} Bias, empirical SE, mean model
  SE, power (type I error under the null), coverage, and
  convergence rate. Each carries its Monte Carlo SE per
  Morris Table 6. With \(n_{\mathrm{sim}} = 2000\), the Monte
  Carlo SE for power at \(p = 0.5\) is
  \(\sqrt{0.5 \times 0.5 / 2000} = 0.011\); for coverage at
  \(0.95\), MCSE \(\approx 0.005\).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data generating model}\label{data-generating-model}

We simulate a two-arm, placebo-controlled trial with
\(n = 200\) participants per arm. Each candidate participant
has a month-0 assessment, which serves as both screening
and randomization visit. Only candidates whose observed
global CDR at month 0 is 0.5 are enrolled. Others are
screened out and replaced, and treatment is assigned after
this step. Follow-up assessments occur at months 6, 12,
18, 24, and 30. Both MMRM outcomes are change from
month 0, a 30-month span. The survival endpoint is the
first follow-up visit at which global CDR is 1.0 or
higher.

The latent continuous disease severity \(Y_{ij}^*\) for
participant \(i\) at visit \(j\) is modeled as:

\[Y_{ij}^* = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t_j + \beta_2 T_i
  + \beta_3 (t_j \times T_i) + b_{0i} + b_{1i} t_j
  + \epsilon_{ij}\]

where \(t_j\) is visit time in years (\(t_0 = 0\)) and \(T_i\)
is the treatment indicator (0 = placebo, 1 = active). The
terms \(b_{0i}\) and \(b_{1i}\) are participant-specific random
intercepts and slopes drawn from a bivariate normal
distribution. Finally, \(\epsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\)
is residual error. Since \(\beta_2 = 0\), the arms do not
differ at month 0.

We obtain the observed global CDR by mapping \(Y^*\) to the
CDR staging categories \{0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3\}. The fixed
thresholds are 0.25, 0.75, 1.5, and 2.5 (\texttt{sim\_trial()}).
These thresholds were chosen to give plausible CDR-stage
proportions and conversion rates for an MCI-due-to-AD
population. We note that the thresholds are a design
choice of this simulation. They were not fit to, or
formally calibrated against, an external dataset such as
ADNI. No sensitivity analysis on these thresholds has yet
been done. We return to this limitation in the
Discussion.

We should be clear about what the latent score is. No
trial observes \(Y^*\), and its residual SD of 0.1 is small
relative to the spread of the random effects. An MMRM on
\(Y^*\) is therefore an analysis of a nearly noise-free
continuous measure. We report it as an approximate upper
bound on the power of a continuous analysis. It is not a model of a
CDR-SB analysis, and CDR-SB is not simulated. The MMRM on
the observed global stage is the comparison that a real
trial could make. It uses the same five-level instrument as
the Cox model.

Screening rests on one noisy month-0 assessment. As a
result, some enrolled participants are close to the
CDR 1.0 threshold at entry and convert at the first
follow-up visit. We report the share who do so in the
Results.

\subsection{Dropout}\label{dropout}

Dropout is monotone. Months 0 and 6 are always observed.
After each follow-up visit \(j\), a participant still in the
trial leaves before the next visit with probability
\(1 - \exp\{-0.075\,[1 + 0.5\,(Y^*_{ij} - 0.5)]\}\). The base
rate is therefore 15\% per year, but the realized rate
varies with severity and so differs by arm. We report the
realized completion fractions in the Results.

This dropout is missing at random given the latent score.
For the MMRM on \(Y^*\) it is therefore ignorable. It is not
ignorable for the other two analyses. Neither the observed
stage nor the time to conversion carries all the
information on which dropout depends. In particular,
censoring in the Cox model depends on severity within a
stage. The independent-censoring assumption of the Cox
model therefore fails\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kalbfleisch2002statistical}{31}}, and
the direction of any resulting bias is not obvious. The
comparison under dropout is thus not symmetric. The MCAR
scenario below removes this asymmetry.

\subsection{Scenarios}\label{scenarios}

The primary scenario uses the values in Table
\ref{tab:sim-params}. Three secondary scenarios each
change one feature of it.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Null.} \(\beta_3 = 0\). Power is then the type I error
  rate of each analysis.
\item
  \textbf{Smaller effect.} \(\beta_3 = -0.0375\), a 12.5\% slowing.
  This value is illustrative. It was chosen to bring the
  latent-score MMRM below its power ceiling.
\item
  \textbf{MCAR dropout.} The primary effect, with dropout at a
  constant 15\% per year that does not depend on severity.
\end{itemize}

Each secondary scenario uses \(R = 500\) replications at
200 per arm. The Monte Carlo SE of a type I error rate
near 0.05 is then about 0.010. No other factor (residual
error, staging thresholds, trial duration) is varied. We
return to this limitation in the Discussion.

\subsection{Design specifications}\label{design-specifications}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Sample size:} 200 per arm (400 total)
\item
  \textbf{Visit schedule:} Month 0 (screening and
  randomization), then months 6, 12, 18, 24, and 30.
\item
  \textbf{Survival endpoint:} Time from randomization to the
  first follow-up visit with global CDR \(\geq\) 1.0
\item
  \textbf{MMRM endpoints:} Change from month 0 to month 30 in
  the latent score and in the observed global CDR stage
\item
  \textbf{Treatment effect:} 25\% slowing of latent progression
  (\(\beta_3 = -0.075\))
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Estimand values}\label{estimand-values}

The latent-score MMRM estimand is
\(\beta_3 \times 2.5 = -0.1875\) by construction. That is,
month 0 precedes randomization, so the arms differ in mean
change at month 30 by the slope difference times
2.5 years. Adjusting for the month-0 value does not change
this target. The other two estimands have no closed form
under this mechanism. We compute them from one very large
simulated trial (200,000 per arm) with no dropout
(\texttt{large\_sample\_targets()}). For the observed stage, the
target is the difference between arms in mean change in
global CDR from month 0 to month 30. It is
-0.1917. For the Cox
model, the target is the large-sample limit of the
estimated log hazard ratio,
-0.3295 (SE
0.0034). The DGM does
not have proportional hazards. This limit is therefore a
population-averaged summary under this design, not a
structural parameter. As a check, the same large trial
gives a latent-score difference of
-0.1865. Under the null
scenario all three targets are zero.

In ICH E9(R1) terms\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ich2019e9r1}{15}}, the MMRM estimands
have four parts. The population is screened participants at
global CDR 0.5. The variable is change from month 0 to
month 30, in the latent score or in the observed stage.
Dropout is the intercurrent event. It is handled under a
hypothetical strategy, that is, the outcome had the
participant stayed in the trial. The likelihood-based MMRM
targets this under MAR. The summary measure is the
difference in means between arms. For the Cox model, the
variable is time to first observed CDR \(\geq 1.0\), dropout
is treated as censoring, and the summary is the hazard
ratio.

Two details of the MMRM deserve mention. First, the
p-value uses a normal reference distribution (a Wald \(z\)
test). With 400 participants this is adequate. We did not
use Kenward-Roger or Satterthwaite degrees of freedom.
Second, the month-0 value is measured before
randomization. As a covariate it improves precision
without changing the target. Its coefficient is allowed
to differ by visit. Under random slopes the regression of
change on the month-0 value is not the same at every
visit. A single pooled coefficient would misspecify the
mean model, and under outcome-dependent dropout that can
bias the treatment contrast.

\subsection{Reproducibility and data/code availability}\label{reproducibility-and-datacode-availability}

All data in this study are simulated. No human-subjects
data are used or accessed. The simulation code
(\texttt{analysis/scripts/sim\_study.R},
\texttt{analysis/scripts/01\_run\_simulation.R},
\texttt{analysis/scripts/02\_power\_vs\_n.R}) and this manuscript
source (\texttt{analysis/report/report.Rmd}) are versioned in the
\texttt{mmrmsurvival} research compendium. The compendium also
holds an \texttt{renv.lock} file pinning package versions.

The functions printed above are read from \texttt{sim\_study.R}
with \texttt{knitr::read\_chunk()} when the document is rendered.
The printed code is therefore the code that runs. Every
number, table, and figure in the Results is computed
during the render, and none is read from a stored file.
Chunk results are cached. The cache key of every chunk
includes an md5 checksum of \texttt{sim\_study.R}, so any change
to the simulation code forces the results to be
recomputed. The RNG kind is pinned to \texttt{L\textquotesingle{}Ecuyer-CMRG}.
Each simulation chunk sets its own seed, so restoring one
chunk from the cache does not change the random numbers
used by later chunks. Each replication has its own
L'Ecuyer stream, derived in sequence from the chunk seed.
Replications run in parallel on 8 cores, and the
results do not depend on the number of cores. A full
render runs about 4,500 replications and takes
substantial time.

Two ADEMP elements are only partly met. First, the
secondary scenarios each change one factor, and they do
not form a full factorial design. Second, the residual
error, the staging thresholds, the dropout-severity
coupling, and the trial duration are fixed (see
Discussion).

\subsection{Sample-size sweep}\label{sample-size-sweep}

In addition to the primary \(n = 200\), \(R = 2000\) design
above, Figure \ref{fig:power-comparison} reports power as
a function of per-arm sample size. These results come from
a separate, lower-precision sweep. \textbf{Aims:} illustrate
how the power of the three analyses changes with sample
size, for trial-planning purposes. \textbf{Data-generating
mechanism:} identical to the primary design
(\texttt{sim\_trial()}), varying only \(n_{\mathrm{per\,arm}}\).
\textbf{Estimands and methods:} identical to the primary
design. \textbf{Performance measure:} power only, with a
95\% Wilson interval. \textbf{\(n\) grid:}
\(n_{\mathrm{per\,arm}} \in \{100, 150, 200, 300, 400\}\), at
\(R = 200\) replications per cell (\texttt{run\_power\_sweep()},
seed 20260511). At \(R = 200\), the Monte Carlo SE for
power near 0.5 is \(\sqrt{0.25/200} \approx 0.035\). That is
roughly three times the primary analysis's value at
\(R = 2000\) (\(\sqrt{0.25/2000} \approx 0.011\)). The sweep is
therefore suitable for the qualitative pattern shown in
Figure \ref{fig:power-comparison}. It is not suitable for
precise point estimates at any single \(n\). The same sweep
can be run outside the report with
\texttt{Rscript\ analysis/scripts/02\_power\_vs\_n.R}.

\subsection{Performance metrics}\label{performance-metrics}

For each method, we compute:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Bias:} Mean estimate minus true parameter value
\item
  \textbf{Empirical SE:} Standard deviation of estimates across
  replications
\item
  \textbf{Mean model-based SE:} Mean of estimated standard errors
\item
  \textbf{Power:} Proportion of replications with \(p < 0.05\)
\item
  \textbf{Coverage:} Proportion of 95\% CIs containing the true
  value
\item
  \textbf{Convergence rate:} Proportion of replications
  producing valid estimates. For the MMRM, `valid'
  requires both no thrown fitting error and genuine
  optimizer convergence. Convergence here means a
  positive-definite approximate Hessian (\texttt{mod\$apVar}) and
  an iteration count below the \texttt{maxIter} cap. The absence
  of an R error alone is not sufficient.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The simulated population}\label{the-simulated-population}

We begin with the simulated population, since it bears on
the Cox results. We first consider conversion to
CDR \(\geq 1.0\) during follow-up. In the primary scenario,
the mean share converting was 83\% in the placebo arm
and 73\% in the active arm. Without
dropout, the corresponding shares in the large trial were
91\% and
81\%. Conversion is therefore
common in this design, and censoring is light. A sizeable
part of it occurs early.
31\% of placebo and
24\% of active participants converted
at the first follow-up visit, month 6. At that visit the
expected latent difference between arms is only
\(-0.0375\). These early conversions depend mainly on where
participants sat within the CDR 0.5 band at entry. The
small early treatment difference shifts them only
slightly. The
share completing the month-30 visit was
70\% (placebo) and
71\% (active). The base rate of
15\% per year alone would give about 74\%. The severity
coupling lowers this in both arms, more so in the placebo
arm, whose participants are more severe on average.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The historical review shows that three reinforcing forces
drove the convergence of AD trials on MMRM and
change-score estimands. The first was the regulatory
requirement for co-primary cognitive and global endpoints,
set by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-leber1990guidelines}{5}}. The global measure was a
clinician's impression of change, not a continuous scale.
Even so, the requirement framed benefit as change over
time rather than as the timing of clinical events. The
second was the decisive empirical evidence against LOCF as
a primary analysis method. That evidence was led by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodt2001dropout}{9}} and codified by the\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-nrc2010missing}{14}} panel. The third was the statistical
efficiency advantage of modeling repeated continuous data
over dichotomizing into time-to-event endpoints. This
advantage was shown by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-donohue2011relative}{25}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-li2019relative}{26}}.

Oncology, by contrast, arrived at a different equilibrium.
Its primary endpoint, death, is inherently binary and
irreversible. The proportional hazards framework has long
regulatory precedent. In addition, no single continuous
severity measure has the role in oncology that ADAS-Cog or
CDR-SB has in AD\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fda2018oncology}{1},\citeproc{ref-fleming1996surrogate}{2}}.

We now turn to the simulation. Its results agree in
direction with the analytic argument of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-donohue2011relative}{25}} and with the simulation results of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-li2019relative}{26}}. Li et al.~simulated from a model fit to
ADNI, with endpoints and censoring patterns unlike ours.
A numerical match with either paper was therefore neither
expected nor attempted.

The more useful result, in our view, is the decomposition.
In the primary scenario, moving from the latent score to
the observed global stage changed MMRM power by
-0.053. Moving from the MMRM on the observed
stage to the Cox model on the same stage changed power by
a further -0.100. The latent-score MMRM is near
its power ceiling in this scenario, so the smaller-effect
scenario is more informative. There, power was
0.666 for the latent-score MMRM,
0.466 for the MMRM on the observed stage, and
0.304 for the Cox model. The latent-score
analysis is not available in practice. Some of the
advantage that a comparison like ours would otherwise
credit to continuous analysis is thus an advantage of a
noise-free measure. Real instruments lie between our two
MMRM analyses. CDR-SB, for example, is finer than the
global stage but is measured with rating error. We have
not simulated it.

But why does the Cox model lose power in this design? The
usual answer is heavy censoring. That answer does not fit
here, since conversion was common and censoring light.
Several other features are at work. First, the Cox model
uses only the time of the first crossing and ignores later
stages. Second, many conversions occur at month 6. At that
visit the expected treatment difference is small. These
events mainly reflect where participants sat within the
CDR 0.5 band at entry. Third, conversion is known only
to the nearest visit, which produces interval-censored
data with many ties. Fourth, dropout depends on severity
within a stage. Censoring is therefore informative for the
Cox model. Under MCAR dropout, Cox power was
0.838, against 0.845 in the
primary scenario. The general cost of reducing a
continuous measure to a threshold is well documented\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-altman2006cost}{32}}.

Under the null scenario, the type I error rates were
0.040,
0.044, and
0.042 for the three
analyses. With 500 replications, observed rates between
about 0.031 and 0.069 are consistent with a true rate of
0.05.

Several caveats apply. First, the comparison is
inherently asymmetric. The MMRM uses more information from
each participant, namely all visits. The Cox model uses
only the first conversion event. Second, the survival
endpoint is limited by the visit schedule. True conversion
may occur between visits. This introduces interval
censoring, which the standard Cox model does not
accommodate. Third, all results rest on one set of
data-generating parameters. We varied the effect size, the
dropout mechanism, and the sample size. We did not vary
the residual error, the staging thresholds, the
dropout-severity coupling, or the trial duration. The
relative efficiency presumably depends on the threshold
chosen. Fourth, the entry rule rests on one assessment.
A trial with a stricter entry rule or a run-in period
would see fewer early conversions. Fifth, the clinical
relevance of the two estimands differs. A treatment effect
expressed as slowing of decline addresses a different
clinical question from one expressed as delay to a
diagnostic milestone. Both may be meaningful to patients
and regulators. The choice between them should be driven
by the estimand framework of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ich2019e9r1}{15}} rather than by
statistical convenience alone.

\section{Future research}\label{future-research}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Missing-not-at-random dropout.} The present
  simulation assumes MAR dropout. Extensions under MNAR
  mechanisms (e.g., pattern-mixture models, selection
  models) would show the robustness of each method under
  more adversarial missingness.
\item
  \textbf{Nonlinear trajectories.} AD progression is not
  strictly linear, particularly in the transition from
  MCI to dementia. Simulation of sigmoidal or piecewise
  trajectories would test the sensitivity of MMRM (which
  makes no linearity assumption) relative to slope-based
  alternatives.
\item
  \textbf{Alternative survival formulations.} Interval-censored
  survival models (e.g.,\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sun1996survival}{33}}) could recover
  some of the information lost by standard Cox
  regression. So could multi-state models of transitions
  among CDR stages (0.5 \(\to\) 1 \(\to\) 2 \(\to\) 3).
  Restricted mean survival time\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-royston2013restricted}{34}}
  is a further alternative. It summarizes the same
  time-to-event data on a continuous, clinically
  interpretable time scale and not as a hazard ratio. It
  would be a natural survival-side comparator in a future
  revision of this simulation. The PMRM\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-raket2022pmrm}{27}}, which also expresses effects as time,
  is a further comparator on the longitudinal side.
\item
  \textbf{Joint modeling.} Joint models of longitudinal CDR
  trajectories and the time-to-conversion event\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rizopoulos2011dynamic}{35}} could unify the two analytic
  approaches. They could potentially offer both the
  efficiency of longitudinal modeling and the clinical
  interpretability of a time-to-event estimand.
\item
  \textbf{Software tools.} An R package implementing the
  simulation framework would help with trial planning.
  It would also provide power calculation utilities for
  both MMRM and survival designs in AD trials.
\item
  \textbf{Applied validation.} Retrospective application of
  both methods to completed trial datasets (e.g., from the
  ADCS or ADNI) would check the simulation findings
  against real data. Such data have genuine missingness
  patterns and measurement properties.
\item
  \textbf{Estimand alignment.} Further work is needed on how
  to apply the ICH E9(R1) estimand framework in one
  setting. In that setting, the same underlying data
  support both change-score and time-to-event estimands.
  A particular issue is the handling of intercurrent
  events such as treatment switching or rescue
  medication.
\end{enumerate}
